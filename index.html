Veteran Property Tax Exemption Navigator

Your state, your rating, your home. Back comes what the exemption is worth this year, the form, the office that takes it, the deadline that applies to you, whether missed years can be recovered, and a filing checklist you can print.

A VA disability rating can cut a veteran's property tax bill to zero in more than twenty states, and reduce it in nearly all the rest. None of it is automatic. The VA does not tell your county, and no state applies the exemption on its own. You file a form with the office that assesses your home: the county appraisal district in Texas, the county property appraiser in Florida, the town assessor in New York and New Jersey, the Chief County Assessment Officer in Illinois, CRIM in Puerto Rico. The deadline is set by the state in some places and by the town in others. Some states refund the years you missed; some do not. Several require an annual renewal form, and a missed renewal is the most common way a veteran loses an exemption already granted. This tool uses the same 120-program data as Best State for Veterans to show what your exemption is worth, then adds the layer no one publishes in one place: the form, the office, the deadline, the lookback rule, the renewal rule, and your state's official office lookup, resolved from your ZIP code, for all 55 jurisdictions.

How this tool works

two data layers · the confidence label · what this isn't

The dollar layer. The value of your exemption comes from the same data that powers Best State for Veterans: 120 property-tax programs across the 50 states, DC, Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and American Samoa, each with its rating threshold, Permanent & Total or TDIU requirement, dollar tiers, income limits, wartime gates, and surviving-spouse rule. The estimate multiplies the exempted value by your state's typical effective property tax rate. Your actual bill uses your assessed value and your local rate, so the office's figure will differ; the estimate exists so you know whether this is a $300 form or a $9,000 form. Where a program has no fixed dollar value (local-option amounts in New York, Rhode Island, and Massachusetts, county programs in Hawaii), the tool shows the rule and no number. One exemption per home is the usual rule, so the verdict uses your best single program.

The filing layer. New for this tool: for every jurisdiction, the office that takes the application, the form name and number with a link to the current version, the deadline type (a fixed date like Florida's March 1, a town-set taxable-status date in New York, county renewal dates in Illinois, a rolling clock in New Jersey), when the exemption takes effect, what happens if you file late, whether prior years can be refunded and under what section, whether you must renew annually or re-file on a rating change or a move, the documents to attach, the appeal path, and your state's official office directory where one exists. A ZIP code resolves your county from the Census Bureau's 2020 ZCTA-to-county file so the directory link and the map search point at your office. For Texas, the card goes one step further and shows the county appraisal district's address, phone, email, and website from the Comptroller's county directory, alongside the tax assessor-collector's details labeled as the office that does not take exemption applications. The deadline card can add the date to your calendar with reminders 30 and 7 days out, because the usual way this benefit is lost is not a denial but a missed date. Every row carries its source links and a verification date. The label on your result says verified when every detail was opened on its official source, reported when key details came only from county pages, and not yet verified when we could not confirm the form or deadline and you should get them from the office in writing.

What this isn't. It is not tax or legal advice, it cannot see your assessment, and it does not file anything for you. Deadlines recur each year and forms get new revision dates, so confirm both with the office before you rely on them. Refunds for missed years are discretionary in several states; the tool says "may" where the law says "may." The filing layer is re-verified each January, when most filing windows open. Data stamp: .
